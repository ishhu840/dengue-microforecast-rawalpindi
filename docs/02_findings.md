# Findings

All numbers below come from the tables in `reports/` and can be regenerated by
running the pipeline in order.

## 1. Reporting delay in Rawalpindi

Onset-to-confirmation interval, 8,433 patients with both dates recorded.

| Statistic | Days |
|---|---|
| Median | 5 |
| Mean | 4.7 |
| 90th percentile | 7 |
| Maximum (after trimming implausible values) | 36 |

| Confirmed within | Share |
|---|---|
| 3 days | 28.9% |
| 7 days | 92.0% |
| 10 days | 98.2% |
| 14 days | 99.5% |

By year: 5.4 days (2021), 4.1 (2022), 3.4 (2023), 5.0 (2024).

**Interpretation.** This is a fast surveillance system. Last week's case counts
are ~92% complete by the following Monday, so a Monday forecast using last
week's counts is working with near-complete data and needs only a light
nowcasting correction. That is an unusually favourable operating condition for
an early-warning system and appears not to be documented elsewhere for
Rawalpindi.

**Caveat.** Onset date is recorded for 54% of patients. If it is recorded
preferentially for patients who present promptly, the true delay is longer than
measured.

## 2. Spatial structure: absent at UC scale, strong at household scale

### 2.1 Moran's I

68 in-season weeks with at least five cases. Mean Moran's I on the log case
surface: **0.260**, significant at p < 0.05 in **78%** of weeks.

Cases are not randomly scattered across the city.

### 2.2 Distance decay — the decisive test

Pairwise correlation of UC weekly counts, by centroid distance:

| Distance | Pairs | Mean correlation |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 km | 320 | 0.287 |
| 2–4 km | 462 | 0.348 |
| 4–6 km | 455 | 0.415 |
| 6–8 km | 349 | 0.389 |
| 8–12 km | 266 | 0.461 |
| 12–20 km | 362 | 0.449 |
| 20+ km | 1,026 | 0.298 |

**There is no decay.** Correlation at 0–2 km (0.287) is essentially the same as
at 20+ km (0.298), and is *lower* than at 8–12 km. If dengue were propagating
between adjacent neighbourhoods, near pairs would be far more correlated than
distant ones. They are not.

At UC resolution, therefore, the clustering measured by Moran's I is not local
transmission. It is **shared seasonal forcing** — the monsoon arrives over the
whole city at once, and every UC rises and falls together — plus the static fact
that high-burden UCs happen to sit near other high-burden UCs.

This conclusion is correct *for this resolution* and it is not the whole story.
Section 2.4 repeats the measurement on household coordinates and reaches the
opposite answer.

### 2.3 The onset test

Among UC-weeks silent for two consecutive weeks in season: 6,269 candidates,
413 lit up the following week (6.6%).

| Fold | n | Onsets | AUC own | AUC own+spatial | ΔAUC | AP own | AP own+spatial | p (permutation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| test 2023 | 1,648 | 97 | 0.829 | 0.819 | −0.010 | 0.268 | 0.238 | 0.061 |
| test 2024 | 1,443 | 108 | 0.868 | 0.874 | +0.006 | 0.431 | 0.408 | 0.227 |

**The neighbour graph does not help.** The two folds disagree in sign, neither
difference is significant, and average precision — the metric that matters when
positives are rare — *falls* in both folds when spatial features are added.

Consistent with this, spatial features account for **4.7%** of total model gain
against **84.9%** for own history and **7.6%** for weather. The model uses
`nb_sum1` (it is the fourth-ranked individual feature) but gains almost nothing
from it.

### 2.4 But the local mechanism is real — it is just smaller than a UC

The distance-decay curve in 2.2 was measured between UC *centroids*, and
Rawalpindi UCs are kilometres across. *Aedes aegypti* disperses 100–200 m. A
genuine local mechanism could operate entirely inside single UCs and be
invisible to any boundary-based analysis.

The 13,850 GPS-tagged patients settle this, because each carries a location and
a date. **Knox's test** asks whether case pairs that are close in space are
*also* close in time more often than chance. This isolates space–time
interaction: cases cluster in space because people live in clusters, and cluster
in time because there is a season, but neither alone implies transmission. The
null permutes dates among locations, so the static population layout and the
epidemic curve are preserved exactly in every replicate.

Ratio of observed to expected close-in-both pairs, pooled over four seasons
(499 permutations, distinct coordinates only):

| Separation | Within 7 days | Within 14 days |
|---|---|---|
| 100 m | **1.80** | 1.47 |
| 200 m | 1.51 | 1.35 |
| 500 m | 1.27 | 1.22 |
| 1000 m | 1.18 | 1.15 |
| 2000 m | 1.09 | 1.08 |

**All 40 tests significant at p = 0.002** (the floor for 499 permutations), in
every season independently.

Two features of this table matter. It **decays monotonically with distance** —
1.80 at 100 m falling to 1.09 at 2 km. And it **decays with time** — at 100 m
the excess is 1.80 within a week but only 1.47 within a fortnight. Both are the
signature of transmission rather than of any static or seasonal artefact.

Data-entry duplication was ruled out first: across 13,850 records the most
patients sharing an exact coordinate is 2, and under 0.2% share a location at
all. The clustering is between genuinely distinct households.

### 2.5 Reconciling 2.2 and 2.4 — the resolution artefact

These two results are not in conflict, and together they are the study's central
finding.

Local transmission is strong at 100–200 m. Union Councils are kilometres wide.
Aggregating cases to UC totals **averages the local signal away**: two houses
40 m apart and two houses 4 km apart land in the same UC-week cell and become
indistinguishable. What survives aggregation is the only thing that operates at
UC scale — the monsoon, arriving over the whole city at once.

So the UC-level graph did not fail because dengue lacks local spatial structure.
It failed because **the analysis unit is ten to twenty times coarser than the
process it was asked to capture.**

### 2.6 What this means for the graph-neural-network literature

The inter-regional dengue GNN work is not contradicted. Between districts of a
country a graph edge represents travelling people, and an infected traveller
genuinely does seed a new region.

The lesson is about **choosing the spatial unit to match the mechanism**. A
graph over administrative boundaries only carries information if those
boundaries are comparable to the dispersal scale of the process. Rawalpindi's
UCs are not, so the graph had nothing to carry — and a study that stopped at
section 2.3 would have concluded, wrongly, that dengue in this city has no local
spatial structure.

The practical rule: **before adopting or rejecting a spatial architecture,
measure the scale at which your process actually operates, and compare it to the
size of your analysis unit.** A Knox test on point data costs very little and
would have changed the conclusion of this study.

## 3. Forecast accuracy

### 3.1 Model comparison

Mean over two held-out seasons, transmission weeks 26–50:

| Model | MAE | RMSE | Poisson dev. | Top-10 hit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Always zero | 1.532 | 6.836 | 69.451 | 30% |
| Persistence | 0.839 | 2.928 | 5.105 | 71% |
| Seasonal UC mean | 1.432 | 6.169 | 5.324 | 60% |
| Static share (deployed app) | 0.889 | 3.113 | 1.202 | 52% |
| XGB own history | 0.831 | 3.335 | 1.011 | 55% |
| XGB own + spatial | 0.828 | 3.323 | 1.000 | 56% |
| **XGB own + weather** | **0.779** | 3.076 | **0.927** | 57% |
| XGB own + spatial + weather | 0.792 | 3.169 | 0.937 | 56% |

Three observations:

- **Weather helps roughly ten times more than space.** Adding weather cuts MAE
  by 6.3%; adding the graph cuts it by 0.4%.
- **The deployed static-share rule is beaten** on Poisson deviance (1.202 vs
  0.927) and on top-10 hit rate — despite being handed the true city total.
- **Persistence has the best top-10 hit rate (71%)** while having a terrible
  Poisson deviance (5.105). It gets the *ranking* right and the *magnitude*
  badly wrong, because the boosters shrink towards the mean and blur the
  ordering. Under weekly retraining the boosters overtake it (§3.2).

### 3.2 The 2024 replay

25 genuine out-of-sample weekly forecasts, models retrained before each week.

| | |
|---|---|
| City MAE | 49.6 cases/week |
| City error at peak season (actual ≥ 10) | 22.2% |
| City 80% interval coverage | 76% |
| Peak week predicted / actual | 42 / 44 |
| Top-10 UC hit rate | **87%** |

The model tracks the season's shape closely: it catches the rise from week 34,
the peak plateau across weeks 41–45, and the decline through week 50. It
under-predicts during the steepest part of the climb (weeks 39–40, 43–44), which
is expected of a one-week-ahead model that learns from the recent level.

The top-10 hit rate rises from 55% (fixed two-fold training) to 87% (weekly
retraining) — the single largest accuracy gain in the study, and it came from
the update schedule rather than the architecture.

### 3.3 How much of the top-10 hit rate is real skill?

The 87% top-10 hit rate needs context, because dengue in Rawalpindi is highly
concentrated and highly persistent:

- The true top-10 UCs hold **88% of all cases** in a typical week.
- The true top-10 list overlaps **83%** with the previous week's.

So "name the same ten UCs as last week" already scores about 83%, and the
deployed app's fixed ranking scores 82.8%. Against those, this model's 87.6% is
an improvement of roughly **five percentage points — about half a UC out of ten
per week.**

That is a real gain and it is worth having, but it is far smaller than the raw
87% suggests. Quoting the headline number without this baseline would overstate
the result. The model's clearer advantages are elsewhere: it flags 42% fewer UCs
for the same job (14.8 vs 9.3 cases caught per UC flagged), and it responds to
the season at all, where a fixed share cannot.

## 4. Uncertainty is calibrated

Negative binomial intervals, dispersion fitted out-of-fold.

| Scope | Nominal | Measured coverage | Mean width |
|---|---|---|---|
| All UC-weeks | 80% | 95.9% | 4.08 |
| All UC-weeks | 95% | 98.8% | 6.79 |
| **UC-weeks with cases** | **80%** | **84.3%** | 14.35 |
| **UC-weeks with cases** | **95%** | **95.5%** | 23.28 |
| City total | 80% | 76.0% | — |

On the scope that matters — UCs actually reporting cases — coverage is close to
nominal in both bands.

The all-UC-weeks rows over-cover because 90% of the grid is zero and an interval
starting at zero cannot be narrowed further; this is a property of discrete
zero-inflated counts, not a tuning failure. City coverage slightly under-covers
because summing UC variances assumes independence between UCs, and a hot season
runs hot everywhere.

## 5. The alert map is actionable

Every UC-week of the replayed season, grouped by the colour shown *before* that
week occurred:

| Alert | UC-weeks | % of map | Mean actual | % with any case | % with 5+ | % of all cases caught |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red | 32 | 1.5% | 68.3 | 100% | 100% | 43.4% |
| Orange | 103 | 4.7% | 17.9 | 100% | 95.1% | 36.5% |
| Yellow | 268 | 12.3% | 2.6 | 81.3% | 17.5% | 13.5% |
| Watch | 106 | 4.9% | 0.7 | 42.5% | 1.9% | 1.5% |
| Green | 1,666 | 76.6% | 0.15 | 10.6% | 0.2% | 5.1% |

**Red and Orange together are 6.2% of the map and contain 79.9% of all cases.**
Every Red and Orange UC-week had at least one case; every Red had five or more.

For a district team with capacity to cover a handful of neighbourhoods per week,
this is the operational result: acting on 6% of the city reaches four cases in
five.

**Onset warning is partial.** Of 78 UCs that reported their first case of the
season while previously silent, 31 were flagged Watch or higher the week before
— a recall of 40%. Useful, clearly imperfect, and honestly the hardest part of
the problem.

## 6. What would improve this most

In order of value per unit of effort:

1. **A weekly case count from the district health office.** Everything here
   works when recent counts exist. Without them the deployed app falls back to a
   weather-only model with R² 0.104. This is a data-access problem and it
   dominates every modelling choice in both studies.
2. **More seasons of GPS-tagged data.** Four years gives two validation folds.
   A fifth and sixth season would let the spatial null result be stated with
   real confidence rather than as an underpowered non-finding.
3. **Investigate the geolocation gap.** A third of Rawalpindi patients have no
   coordinate, and coverage swings from 66% to 94% by year. If tagging is not
   missing-at-random across neighbourhoods, the UC shares carry that bias.
4. **Forecast below UC resolution.** Sections 2.4–2.5 show that real predictive
   signal is discarded by aggregating to UCs. A continuous risk surface built
   from household points — or a grid of a few hundred metres — should outperform
   the UC panel, because it would operate at the scale the process actually runs
   at. This is the most promising modelling direction the study opens, and
   section 7 shows the operational payoff is already visible with no model at
   all.


## 7. Micro-hotspot targeting

Sections 2.4–2.5 have a direct operational consequence. If cases cluster within
100–200 m of a recent case, a ring drawn around every case reported this week
should contain a disproportionate share of next week's.

Measured over 83 consecutive in-season week pairs, 2021–2024. Overlapping rings
are merged so shared ground is paid for once. Rawalpindi Tehsil is 1,632 km².

| Strategy | Catches | Area | Share of tehsil | Cases caught per km² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m rings | 19.7% | 4.3 km² | 0.3% | **4.6** |
| 200 m rings | 42.3% | 14.3 km² | 0.9% | 3.0 |
| 500 m rings | 72.9% | 55.2 km² | 3.4% | 1.3 |
| UC map, Red + Orange | 79.8% | 90.8 km² | 5.6% | 0.9 |

At peak weeks (50+ cases reported), 500 m rings catch 75.1% over 89.9 km²
against the UC map's 79.8% over 90.8 km² — near-identical at equal area.

**The two approaches are not competing; they answer different budgets.**

- With capacity for a few km² a week, rings around last week's cases are **five
  times more efficient per km²** than the UC map. 200 m rings reach 42% of next
  week's cases across 0.9% of the district.
- With capacity for ~90 km², the UC map is the better instrument and reaches
  roughly 80%.

Note that the ring strategy requires **no model at all** — only last week's
geocoded case list. It is deployable immediately, and it is a fair baseline that
any future fine-scale model should have to beat.

## 8. Summary of the spatial argument

1. Cases cluster spatially at UC level (Moran's I 0.26, 78% of weeks).
2. But that clustering does not decay with distance between UCs, and a
   neighbour graph adds nothing to UC-level forecasts.
3. At household scale it decays sharply — 1.80× excess at 100 m within 7 days,
   falling to 1.09× at 2 km, p = 0.002 in every season independently.
4. Therefore the UC-level null is a **resolution artefact**, not an absence of
   spatial structure. The analysis unit is 10–20× coarser than the process.
5. The discarded signal is operationally valuable: rings around last week's
   cases are five times more area-efficient than UC-level alerting.
